Reflection: Looking Beyond Oil Prices

Published July 3 2026

When I first looked into the Strait of Hormuz disruption, I mainly thought about it from a macro perspective. My thinking was simple: if a major supply route is disrupted, oil prices go up, inflation stays higher, and the market starts expecting interest rates to remain elevated for longer. I assumed that oil prices were the main variable driving the story.

After writing my note, I had the chance to speak with Jeff Gordon from Chevron. One question I asked was whether people inside the industry also look at oil prices first when something like Hormuz happens. His answer was interesting because he said they actually start somewhere else. Before looking at prices, they try to understand the physical market. They ask questions like what type of crude is affected, whether refineries can process alternative grades, where replacement supply can come from, and how quickly shipping routes can adjust. In other words, prices are often the result of what happens in the physical market rather than the starting point.

A few days later, I read a Reuters article about ENEOS, Japan’s largest oil refiner, and it reminded me of our conversation. Instead of focusing on higher oil prices, the company talked about securing replacement crude from the U.S. and Azerbaijan and finding shipping routes that avoided the Strait of Hormuz. What stood out to me was that their first priority wasn’t predicting where oil prices would go—it was figuring out how to keep the supply chain functioning.

That changed how I think about similar macro events. Now, when I read headlines about an oil supply disruption, I don’t immediately jump to inflation or interest rates. I first ask whether the physical supply chain can adapt. If refiners can find substitute crude and shipments can be rerouted, then the economic impact may end up being much smaller than the initial headlines suggest. I still think prices matter, but now I see them more as the outcome of what’s happening underneath, rather than the starting point of the analysis.
